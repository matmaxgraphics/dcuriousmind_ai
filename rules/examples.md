# Examples

Real d_CuriousMind posts. Match this register — not the topic, the *feel*.

## Good openings

> Why do cocks crow? 🐓
> Have you ever stopped to ask why cocks crow? Funny question, right?
>
> You might say, "Oh, their crowing is basically an internal alarm clock to
> wake humans up in the morning."
>
> But how do you explain why they crow in the afternoon?

> Have you ever wondered why we shiver when cold — is it just a random
> response to a temperature drop, or does it serve a purpose?

> Does fire really have a shadow?
> Fire is a source of light itself, and it's only logical that it cannot cast
> a shadow since it's not an opaque object. But if we look at the image
> below, the fire on the candle appears to cast a shadow on the floor. Why is
> that?

> Have you ever wondered why ice makes those cracking sounds? You might have
> noticed while placing your ice in a glass of wine, or while pouring water
> on ice cubes, you hear this sound that feels like the ice is breaking.

## What these share

1. Open with the question, not the answer.
2. State the assumption the reader already holds.
3. Puncture that assumption with an observation it cannot explain
   ("but why do they crow in the afternoon?").
4. Only then explain the mechanism.
5. Address the reader directly — "you might say", "have you ever noticed".
6. The phenomenon is ordinary. Only the explanation is surprising.

## Topics to avoid

These are real examples the pipeline wrongly accepted. They are fascinating
and still wrong for us — they report something new to the world instead of
explaining something familiar to the reader:

- "The Brain May Be Two Organs, a Discovery That Could Advance ALS Research"
- "Cub Found in Bolivia Helped Uncover the First New Cat Species in a Century"
- "9-Million-Year-Old Capybara Tooth Hints at Wet Conditions in the Atacama"
- "A Herd of 40 Life-Size Bison Puppets Will Stampede Through New York"
